# Existing Books Adjacent to "Metasystems Engineering" and the Gap a New Book Could Fill

Scope note: "Metasystems engineering" is used here in three senses: (a) governing and coordinating systems of systems (systems engineering, cybernetics, Stafford Beer); (b) systems that build systems (software factories, platform engineering, developer productivity, AI agents that build software); (c) the operating systems of organizations and individuals (management systems, operating models, personal systems). Everything below was retrieved on 2026-09-17 unless a different date is given. Goodreads figures marked "(snippet)" come from search-engine summaries of Goodreads pages. Figures marked "(fetched)" were read directly from the page. All counts shift over time and differ by edition, so treat them as approximate. A dagger (†) marks a publication year, publisher or one-line thesis for an older classic that comes from the researcher's background knowledge and was not re-checked this session. Confirm those before putting them in a proposal. The a/b/c tags are the researcher's own classification.

## Q1. Which existing books sit closest, and what do they cover and how were they received?

### Takeaway
About 34 books sit near the topic, and they split cleanly into three groups: cybernetics and systems theory (a), builder-layer practice (b), and operating-model or personal-system books (c). No book found covers all three. The only book that literally uses the term, Hall's *Metasystems Methodology* (1989), has almost no readers: 2 Goodreads ratings and 0 reviews. The strongest recent comparable titles ("comps") are *Team Topologies* 2e (2025), *Platform Engineering* (2024), *The Unaccountability Machine* (2024), *Vibe Coding* (2025) and *Staff Engineer* (2021). Two August 2026 releases are the closest direct competitors: Osmani's *Agentic Engineering* and von Grünberg & Galante's *Thinking in Platforms*.

### Inferences
- **Coverage matrix (researcher's classification of the 34 rows):** a only: Hall, Meadows, Gall, INCOSE, Maier/Rechtin. a + c: Beer ×2, Senge, Gharajedaghi, Davies, Kim/Spear (c + a). a + b: Montalion only (3.37, the lowest-rated book in the set), with Maier/Rechtin partly. b + c: Ohno, Womack, *Phoenix*, *Unicorn*, *Accelerate*, *Team Topologies*, *Platform Engineering*, *Platform Strategy*, *Thinking in Platforms*, *Frictionless*, *Elegant Puzzle*. b only: *SWE at Google*, *DDIA*, *Vibe Coding*, *Agentic Engineering*, Merced. c only: *The Goal*, Grove, *Working Backwards*, *Staff Engineer*, *Building a Second Brain*, *Atomic Habits*. **a + b + c: none found.**
- **Suggested comparable titles** (recent, trade-leaning, with visible reception): *Team Topologies* 2e (IT Revolution 2025), *Platform Engineering* (O'Reilly 2024), *The Unaccountability Machine* (Profile/UChicago 2024), *Vibe Coding* (IT Revolution 2025), *Wiring the Winning Organization* (IT Revolution 2023) and *Staff Engineer* (2021, for the interview format). Treat *Agentic Engineering* (Aug 2026) and *Thinking in Platforms* (Aug 2026) as direct competitors to position against, not as sales comps, because they are too new for sales signals. Present classics (*Thinking in Systems*, *The Goal*, *Brain of the Firm*) as the tradition the book belongs to rather than as comps, since most proposal guidance favors comps from the last ~5 years (general industry practice, not sourced here).
- The closest positioning threat is *Thinking in Platforms*. It already pitches platform engineering as the operating model for all knowledge work in the AI era (b + c). Its launch material leaves out cybernetics and governance theory (a) and personal systems, which leaves the user's a + b + c angle open.
- Osmani's *Agentic Engineering* takes the "software factory" framing from the individual engineer's side, so a metasystems book should come at the factory from governance and organization design rather than from agent-workflow technique.

## Q2. What complaints come up again and again in reviews of these books?

### Takeaway
Seven complaints recur: (1) diagnosis without anything to act on, or too abstract; (2) superficial or "common sense" for experienced readers; (3) a loose collection of blog posts rather than a book; (4) repetitive and padded; (5) too much jargon or too many named concepts; (6) unrealistic or preachy stories; (7) vendor-flavored content in the platform and AI-factory space. The academic systems and cybernetics books mainly suffer from reaching almost nobody (tiny readerships, out of print).

### Cited Findings
- **Diagnosis without prescription / too abstract:** Goodreads reviewers of *The Unaccountability Machine* say its solutions are weak and abstract, its cybernetics sections feel detached from practice, and its narrative wanders ([Goodreads, fetched](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/197716282-the-unaccountability-machine)). A practitioner reviewer praised the diagnosis but was disappointed on what to actually do ([Earnestness Is Underrated](https://earnestnessisunderrated.substack.com/p/book-review-the-unaccountability)).
- **Superficial / common sense:** *Platform Engineering* reviewers say experienced leaders will find much of it common sense and some call it "too superficial"; its focus is soft skills and the big picture rather than technical depth ([Goodreads, fetched](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/217312157-platform-engineering)). *Vibe Coding* reviewers say it adds little beyond what hands-on developers already work out and is light on rigor ([Goodreads, fetched](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/228438060-vibe-coding)).
- **Disjointed collection:** *An Elegant Puzzle* is criticized for a loose structure with no coherent narrative and for oversimplifying ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/45303387-an-elegant-puzzle)). *Building a Second Brain* reads to some like a blog post turned into a book ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/59616977-building-a-second-brain)).
- **Repetitive / padded:** *Vibe Coding* is called repetitive and bloated, and some suspect it was AI-written; reviewers also say it swings between beginner and advanced material ([Goodreads, fetched](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/228438060-vibe-coding)). *Building a Second Brain* is called verbose ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/59616977-building-a-second-brain)). *Wiring the Winning Organization* is called repetitive, oversimplified, and hard to apply without senior buy-in ([SoBrief aggregator](https://sobrief.com/books/wiring-the-winning-organization)). *Tribe of Mentors* is called too long ([Goodreads editions](https://www.goodreads.com/work/editions/57830167-tribe-of-mentors)).
- **Jargon and concept density:** On a June 2026 Hacker News thread about applying *Team Topologies* to agentic platforms, a commenter complained of about three named concepts per paragraph ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48640382)). *The Unicorn Project* is criticized for heavy technical jargon ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/44333183-the-unicorn-project)).
- **Unrealistic or preachy stories:** *The Unicorn Project* is criticized for an unrealistic picture of corporate transformation, thin characters, glorifying overtime, and a preachy tone. Some IT readers found *The Phoenix Project* pedantic ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/44333183-the-unicorn-project); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/38247436)).
- **Writing quality:** One review of *Team Topologies* praises its theory but found basic writing errors frustrating ([BookAnon](https://bookanon.com/2023/12/31/bookreview-team-topologies-by-matthew-skelton-and-manuel-pais/)).
- **Theory that doesn't hold in practice:** An HN commenter said *Team Topologies* sounds sensible but has not worked in their experience ([HN search summary](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=36903833)). A May 2025 essay, "The fundamental misunderstanding in Team Topologies", drew little engagement: 2 points, 1 comment ([HN, fetched](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44117034)).
- **Vendor-flavored material in the platform and software-factory space:** *Thinking in Platforms* comes from an author whose bio says he founded platform-orchestrator companies ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Thinking-Platforms-Platform-engineering-operating/dp/3982887720); [platformengineering.org author page](https://platformengineering.org/authors/kaspar-von-gruenberg)). *Frictionless* is co-written by the CEO of DX, which sells developer-experience measurement ([DX](https://getdx.com/news/frictionless/)). Most "what is a software factory" explainers in 2026 come from vendors: Augment Code, Cortex, Factory.ai, TrueFoundry, Opsera ([Augment](https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/what-is-a-software-factory); [Cortex](https://www.cortex.io/post/what-is-an-ai-software-factory); [Factory.ai](https://factory.ai/news/software-factory); [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/software-factory-agentic-enterprise-guide); [Opsera](https://opsera.ai/blog/secure-software-factory/)).
- **Academic, niche, out of print:** Hall's *Metasystems Methodology* has 2 Goodreads ratings and is mostly found used or on Internet Archive ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/3534708-metasystems-methodology); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/metasystemsmetho0000hall)). Beer's *Brain of the Firm* has only 137 ratings across editions ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/editions/1293720-brain-of-the-firm-classic-beer-series)).
- **Systems thinking for software under-delivers:** Montalion's *Learning Systems Thinking* averages 3.37 across 100 ratings, the lowest in the set ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/205977642-learning-systems-thinking)).
- **Gap between theory and practice in systems thinking:** readers are pointed to "practical" follow-ups such as *Systems Thinking Made Simple* (Cabrera; some find it oversimplified) and *Methods for Systems Thinking: 65+ Practical Approaches*, which suggests the classic primers leave readers wanting methods ([search summary of Goodreads lists](https://www.goodreads.com/shelf/show/systems-thinking)).
- **Internal-platform failures are documented in articles, not books:** common failure modes are building before understanding developer needs, forcing adoption (which leads to "malicious compliance"), and running the platform as an infrastructure project instead of a product ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/articles/platform-engineering-lessons-learned); [Romaric Philogene, Platform Tips #15](https://romaricphilogene.substack.com/p/platform-tips-15-dont-be-fooled-by)).

### Inferences
- A new book can turn these complaints into its pitch: **real named cases, including failures** (answers "no real cases" and "common sense"); **actionable operating mechanisms** (answers "diagnosis without prescription"); **one through-line** (answers "blog-post collection"); **vendor-neutral** (answers vendor flavor); **a small, disciplined vocabulary** (answers jargon density).
- Books rated below 3.8 in this set (*Vibe Coding* 3.71, *Unaccountability Machine* 3.74, Montalion 3.37, *97 Things* 3.62) share a pattern: an ambitious framing with thin or scattered execution. A book that blends three meanings of a term is exposed to the same risk.

### Gaps
- No Reddit threads (r/ExperiencedDevs, r/systems_engineering, r/systemsthinking) were read directly. Searches returned vendor and InfoQ articles instead, so the Reddit view is missing.
- Why Montalion's book is rated low was not found (reviews not fetched).
- *Thinking in Systems* and *The Fifth Discipline* reviews were not read; the "too abstract" complaint about them is unconfirmed.
- *Team Topologies* Goodreads reviews could not be read (the fetch returned an empty page).

## Q3. Which topics fall between these books, where no single book connects them? (the gap)

### Takeaway
There is solid evidence that nothing connects **Beer-style metasystem governance (a)** with the **2024–2026 builder layer of internal platforms, software factories and AI coding agents (b)**, and no book carries the same model **down to personal operating systems and up to the whole organization (c)**. Practitioners make the a × b connection only in blog posts, white papers and training PDFs. The 2026 books on the builder layer (Osmani; von Grünberg & Galante) do not use cybernetics. The gap is also well timed: 2026 is Beer's centenary, the "software factory" metaphor was revived in the first half of 2026, and the builder layer's bottleneck has moved to verification and governance.

### Cited Findings
- **The a × b link lives only in blogs, white papers and training material.** Jessica Kerr (Jessitron) maps the VSM onto software companies, with development teams as System 1 and platform teams as System 2 ([Jessitron, 2022](https://jessitron.com/2022/08/28/the-viable-systems-model-and-where-my-team-fits/)). The Agile Business Consortium has a white paper on scaling agile with the VSM ([Agile Business](https://www.agilebusiness.org/resource-report/applying-the-viable-system-model-to-the-challenge-of-scaling-agile.html)). Krishan Mathis has a training PDF on the VSM for lean-agile practice ([vsm-training.org](https://vsm-training.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/viable-system-model-en.pdf)). Umbrex maps VSM subsystems onto DevOps and SRE work (S2 incident management, S3 policy-as-code and error budgets, algedonic signals for escalation) ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/viable-system-model-stafford-beer/)). A targeted search for a book applying the VSM to software, agile or DevOps turned up only these (search 2026-09-17).
- **Agentic-platform org design is being worked out without cybernetics.** Olivier Wulveryck's June 2026 post applies *Team Topologies* to AI agents. The platform takes on context, guardrails and tooling so business teams can direct agents, and the human's job becomes anticipating everything upfront in a single prompt. It cites only *Team Topologies* and never mentions Beer, the VSM or cybernetics ([Wulveryck blog, fetched](https://blog.owulveryck.info/2026/06/22/who-does-what-team-topologies-for-the-agentic-platform.html)).
- **The builder layer's bottleneck is now governance and verification.** Augment Code (Aug 2026) defines a software factory as an agent-run delivery operating model where humans keep control of specifications and merge approval. It names verification as the binding constraint and calls the factory "a coordination problem before it is a tooling one" ([Augment, fetched](https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/what-is-a-software-factory)). Several 2026 explainers say the constraint has moved to verification, security, governance and long-term ownership, with the governance loop still open ([search summary of TrueFoundry, Opsera and others](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/software-factory-agentic-enterprise-guide)).
- **The "software factory" has a long history that no book ties to cybernetics.** The term traces from R.W. Bemer's proposal at the 1968 NATO conference, to Hitachi Software Works (1969), Michael Cusumano's studies of Japanese software factories (1989–1991), and Microsoft's 2004 revival by Greenfield and Short ([Augment, fetched](https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/what-is-a-software-factory)). In the first half of 2026, BCG Platinion used "Agentic Software Factory" in March and Factory.ai published its "Factory 2.0" manifesto in June ([search summary](https://factory.ai/news/software-factory)). Tesla's "machine that builds the machine" is the manufacturing version of the idea ([Harvard D3 case](https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-rctom/submission/tesla-building-the-machine-that-builds-the-machine)).
- **The accountability concept exists but has not reached engineering.** Davies' "accountability sinks" (organizations that diffuse accountability through process) resonated with readers ([Goodreads, fetched](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/197716282-the-unaccountability-machine)), yet no book in the set applies it to agent pipelines or internal platforms.
- **The newest b + c book leaves out a and the personal level.** *Thinking in Platforms* extends platform engineering to all knowledge work, but its launch post says nothing about AI agents building software, cybernetics or Beer ([launch post, fetched](https://kasparvongruenberg.substack.com/p/my-new-book-thinking-in-platforms)). *Agentic Engineering* runs "from individual workflow to the software factory" from the engineer's side ([O'Reilly listing, snippet](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/agentic-engineering/0642572392291/)).
- **Personal systems live in their own genre.** *Atomic Habits* (systems over goals; ~256k ratings) and *Building a Second Brain* (~21.6k ratings) are about individual operating systems and have no link to organizational or engineering metasystems ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40121378-atomic-habits); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/59616977-building-a-second-brain)).
- **The market is active and the timing works.** *Team Topologies* 2e (Sep 2025) ([IT Revolution](https://itrevolution.com/product/team-topologies-second-edition/)), *Vibe Coding* (Oct 2025), *DDIA* 2e (Mar 2026), *Agentic Engineering* (Aug 2026) and *Thinking in Platforms* (Aug 2026) all appeared within 12 months, and 2026 is Beer's centenary, which Metaphorum is marking ([Metaphorum](https://metaphorum.org/)).
- **Multi-agent governance is an open, recognized problem.** Governance and observability are described as the biggest 2026 agent-development challenges, NIST launched an AI Agent Standards Initiative in February 2026, and a Gartner-attributed forecast says 40%+ of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 over governance and ROI ([search summary, dev.to / RTInsights](https://www.rtinsights.com/if-2025-was-the-year-of-ai-agents-2026-will-be-the-year-of-multi-agent-systems/)). That summary does not mention the VSM ([same search](https://dev.to/tamizuddin/the-2026-ai-agent-stack-from-local-execution-to-governance-layer-2dc9)).

### Inferences
Six unconnected topics a "metasystems engineering" book could own:
1. **Cybernetic governance of the builder layer.** Apply the VSM (operations, coordination, control, intelligence, identity) to internal platforms and AI-agent fleets: who sets policy, who audits, and which escalation signals (Beer's "algedonic" alerts) reach leadership. Today this exists only in blogs and white papers.
2. **Accountability in automated production.** Combine Davies' accountability sinks with the finding that verification is now the binding constraint, to answer who is accountable when the factory ships something wrong.
3. **Failure stories from meta-systems.** Internal-platform failures (malicious compliance, low adoption) and agent-factory failures are documented in articles, not books. Collecting practitioner accounts would answer the "no real cases / common sense" complaint.
4. **One model at every scale.** Show the same patterns at the level of the individual (personal operating system), the team, the organization and the agent fleet. No book goes from *Atomic Habits* to *Brain of the Firm* to an agentic software factory.
5. **A single lineage.** Toyota Production System → Japanese software factories → DevOps → platform engineering → agentic software factories, told alongside cybernetics as one history of systems that build systems.
6. **Measuring the metasystem.** Connect DORA and developer-experience metrics (*Accelerate*, *Frictionless*) with cybernetic ideas of requisite variety and feedback. No source seen in this research does.

Main risks:
- The "b" lane got crowded in August 2026.
- "Metasystem" means nothing to practitioners (see Q4), so the subtitle will have to explain the idea.
- A three-way blend runs the same risk of scattered execution that pulled down the ratings of other ambitious books (Q2).

### Gaps
- Whether *Team Topologies*, *Platform Engineering*, *Accelerate*, *Wiring the Winning Organization* or *Thinking in Platforms* reference Beer or the VSM inside the book was not checked; only launch material and reviews were seen. Checking indexes or full text would firm up the a × b claim.
- Absence of evidence is not conclusive. The searches were English-language, US-indexed web searches and did not cover academic monographs (for example Springer or Emerald titles on organizational cybernetics) or non-English books.
- The claim that nothing spans personal to organizational to agent systems is an inference from the 34 books reviewed, not from an exhaustive catalog search.

## Q4. Is anyone already using "metasystem" or "meta-engineering" framing for practitioners?

### Takeaway
No practitioner book, newsletter or course using "metasystems engineering" turned up. The term is unclaimed but also unknown, and "meta-engineering" mostly returns results about Meta (the company) or metaprogramming. Nearby communities do exist: organizational cybernetics (Metaphorum, the Tektological Substack, Dan Davies' Substack), the vendor-led "software factory" conversation, and bloggers mapping the VSM or *Team Topologies* onto platforms and agents.

### Cited Findings
- A search for "metasystems engineering" newsletters, Substacks or courses returned only generic engineering-newsletter lists, none using the term ([Substack Technology](https://substack.com/top/technology); [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/)).

A subtitle carrying one or more of those terms would help (researcher's inference).

### Gaps

- Existing trademarks or domains using "metasystems engineering" were not checked.

## Q5. Which crowdsourced-wisdom books built from many practitioners' experiences have worked, and how were they structured and received?

### Takeaway
Four structures show up: (1) many short essays gathered through an open wiki (*97 Things*, 3.62 on Goodreads); (2) long single-person interview chapters (*Coders at Work*, 3.95 / ~5.4k ratings); (3) the same questions sent to many people (*Tribe of Mentors*, ~4.05 / ~15k ratings on the Kindle edition, criticized as shallow); (4) a framework plus curated interviews or cases (*Staff Engineer*, 4.04 / ~3k ratings; *Team Topologies* 2e's new case appendix). In this small sample, the framework-plus-cases hybrid gets the best balance of rating and credibility with practitioners, and pure short-essay anthologies rate lowest.

### Cited Findings
- ***97 Things Every Software Architect Should Know*** (ed. Richard Monson-Haefel; O'Reilly, 2009): short pieces collected and refined on a public wiki, each licensed CC Attribution 3.0, from more than four dozen architects including Neal Ford and Michael Nygard, then published by O'Reilly ([InfoQ, 2008](https://www.infoq.com/news/2008/08/97things); [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/97-things-every/9780596800611/); [Erik Dörnenburg, 2009](https://erik.doernenburg.com/2009/03/97-things/)). The number 97 came from a conversation between Monson-Haefel and Jeremy Meyer ([Kevlin Henney, "FAQ: Why 97 Things?"](https://kevlinhenney.medium.com/faq-why-97-things-825ce0fc7862)). Goodreads 3.62 / 788 ratings (snippet) ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/5487765-97-things-every-software-architect-should-know)).
- ***Coders at Work*** (Peter Seibel; 2009): 15 long interviews, one programmer per chapter, covering how each started, how they debug, their design philosophy and career turning points. Over 600 pages, and each chapter stands alone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coders_at_Work); [DEV index of the 15 interviews](https://dev.to/soytuber/coders-at-work-index-of-all-15-programmer-interviews-kcf)). Goodreads 3.95 / 5,381 ratings (snippet) ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/6713575-coders-at-work)). It follows the earlier *Programmers at Work* (19 interviews) ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Programmers-Work-Interviews-Computer-Industry/dp/1556152116)).
- ***Tribe of Mentors*** (Tim Ferriss; 2017): the same 11 questions sent to hundreds of people, hoping for ~100 replies and getting 140+. Goodreads Kindle edition 4.05 / 15,383 ratings; hardcover 4.33 / 1,362. Criticized as long, shallow (most profiles run 3–4 pages) and recycled from his podcast. Praised because nearly every reader finds some mentor who speaks to them ([Goodreads editions](https://www.goodreads.com/work/editions/57830167-tribe-of-mentors); [StoryGraph reviews](https://app.thestorygraph.com/book_reviews/ba4eefb1-a86d-47e0-8992-d8cada4a6a20?sort=lowest_rated&page=9)).
- ***Staff Engineer*** (Will Larson; 2021): Part 1 synthesizes material on the Staff+ role gathered from many companies; Part 2 is interviews with Staff+ engineers about how they sponsor, mentor and spend their weeks. It grew out of the staffeng.com site. Goodreads 4.04 / ~3,046 ratings (snippet) ([staffeng.com/book](https://staffeng.com/book/); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/56481725-staff-engineer)).
- ***Seeking SRE: Conversations About Running Production Systems at Scale*** (curated by David N. Blank-Edelman; O'Reilly, 2018†): more than two dozen chapters framed as conversations from practitioners across many organizations ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/seeking-sre/9781491978856/); [ebooks.com](https://www.ebooks.com/en-us/book/96332658/seeking-sre/david-n-blank-edelman/)). Rating not retrieved.
- ***Team Topologies*, 2nd ed.** (2025): the update's selling point is an appendix of real-world cases from the global community (ING Netherlands, KFC UK&I, Creditas, Yassir). IT Revolution marketed it as "real-world lessons from the global business community" ([IT Revolution](https://itrevolution.com/articles/team-topologies-2nd-edition-real-world-lessons-from-the-global-business-community/)).
- ***An Elegant Puzzle*** is a single-author collection of articles in five sections and is criticized as disjointed ([Goodreads snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/45303387-an-elegant-puzzle)). This is a warning for any book assembled from separate pieces.

### Inferences
- Among engineering titles, the formats that give each practitioner real depth (*Coders at Work*, *Staff Engineer*) outrate the short-essay anthology (*97 Things*). *Tribe of Mentors* shows the many-short-answers format can sell hugely on the strength of the author's platform but draws "shallow" complaints. The sample is small and confounded by author fame and publication era, so this is only suggestive.
- A strong structure for the user's book: an original framework running through the whole book (the three meanings joined by one metasystem model), with each chapter built on a few curated practitioner accounts, including failures, plus a case appendix. That borrows the strengths of *Staff Engineer* and *Team Topologies* 2e and avoids the loose-collection complaint.
- *97 Things* shows one way to handle rights for community material: an explicit open license (CC-BY) plus contributor attribution. A book built from experiences people shared online will need its own permissions approach (researcher's inference; not legal advice).

### Gaps
- Goodreads ratings for *Seeking SRE*, *97 Things Every Programmer Should Know* and the rest of the *97 Things* series were not retrieved.
- Sales figures (as opposed to rating counts) for any crowdsourced title were not found.
- How *Staff Engineer* and *Tribe of Mentors* handled contributor permissions or compensation was not researched.
- Other possible models not examined: *Founders at Work* (Livingston), *Tools of Titans* (Ferriss), *97 Things Every Engineering Manager Should Know*.
