# The Fun Toolkit — making *Every Good Regulator* a joy to read

Companion to `BOOK_PLAN.md` (section 5 there covers structure; this file covers delight). Written 25 September 2026 after studying the books in this folder.

**The rule that governs everything below: every joke carries a fact.** Munroe's books are funny *because* the science underneath is right. Nothing here is decoration — each device either teaches the idea, makes it memorable, or gives the reader a breather before the next hard section.

---

## 1. What each book taught us

| Book | The device worth stealing (the idea, never the content) |
|---|---|
| **Munroe, *What If?*** | A real question, answered by walking through each subsystem in turn, with one delightful true fact per section and a deadpan one-line joke closing the section. Jokes live in the footnotes. Between chapters, **"Weird (and Worrying) Questions from the Inbox"** pages: three reader questions with names, one stick-figure gag, and no answer at all — pure breathing room. |
| **Munroe, *How To*** | An everyday goal solved by taking it absurdly seriously — with real equations and real data tables sitting next to the jokes. Short **unnumbered interlude chapters** between the numbered ones ("How to Listen to Music", "How to Chase a Tornado"). |
| **Munroe, *Thing Explainer*** | A hard constraint: explain everything using only the **thousand most common English words**. And **huge labelled blueprint diagrams** — a cutaway of a sailing ship, dozens of dashed arrows with captions, tiny stick figures and a sea monster hidden in the margins as rewards for looking closely. |
| ***xkcd*, volume 0** | Hand-drawn stick figures on grid paper. The comic timing trick: a **silent final panel** where the punchline happens without words. |
| **Henry (ed.), *Eating Salad Drunk*** | **Haikus from many different contributors**, one per page, each credited — a crowdsourced collection. The 5-7-5 form forces compression, and the last line turns the joke. (Its content is very adult — a format model only.) |
| **Foer, *Moonwalking with Einstein*** | **The author becomes the experiment**: a journalist covering the memory championship trains for a year and competes. History and science are woven through a personal quest. Homemade units of measure — a 924-pound deadlift is "about thirty of my nieces". |
| **Manson, *The Subtle Art…*** | Talks straight to *you*. One running motif carried through the whole book. Counterintuitive chapter titles that sound like provocations. |
| **Greene, *33 Strategies of War* and *Mastery*** | A **fixed chapter template**: historical stories → **"Keys"** (the analysis) → **"Image"** (one memorable metaphor) → **"Authority"** (a quote from history) → **"Reversal"** (when the opposite is true). *Mastery* mixes historical figures with **interviews of living practitioners** — the same shape this book needs. |
| **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*** | Numbered, short, quotable principles. Readers remember and share them. |
| **Dixit, Skeath & McAdams, *Games of Strategy*** | Textbook discipline: a **summary**, **key terms**, **solved** and **unsolved exercises** at the end of every chapter, and hundreds of diagrams. |
| **Hackett, "The Tai Lung Journey" (movie-therapy paper)** | A **film character as a case study** — using a story people already know to teach an idea. |
| **Wells, *The Time Machine*** | A speculative story told as a first-person report from the future. (Not opened here; the structure is well known.) |
| ***Dark Psychology 2 in 1*** | The counter-example: sweeping claims, no sources, padding. Exactly what the research found gets books rated below 3.8. |

## 2. The toolkit — fourteen devices, adapted

### A. Haikus — the "Haiku Line"
One haiku closes each chapter (never in the serious ones — see rules in section 4). Original examples, all 5-7-5:

> *Straight rows, perfect count.*
> *The ledger balanced for years.*
> *Then the forest died.*
> — Chapter 1, The Forest That Died

> *Watch the robot work.*
> *All day it does not fail once.*
> *It fails. Where were you?*
> — Chapter 10, The Ironies of Automation

> *We measured the tests.*
> *Coverage hit one hundred.*
> *Nothing is tested.*
> — Chapter 5, Compliant and Fatal

> *The cord hangs right there.*
> *Everyone knows you can pull.*
> *No one ever has.*
> — Chapter 12, Who May Stop the Line

> *My second brain grew.*
> *I now spend all of Sunday*
> *tending to its needs.*
> — Chapter 15, Your Own Operating System

**The crowdsourced version (from *Eating Salad Drunk*):** invite engineers, nurses, pilots, maintainers and managers to submit haikus about the systems they work inside. Publish the best, credited, in a **Haiku Wall** appendix. This does three jobs at once — it is fun, it delivers the "many people's experiences" you wanted from the start, and it builds an audience before launch. Contributors need to sign a simple permission form.

### B. "What If?" chapters and interludes
Four or five short interlude chapters between the main ones, each a single absurd-sounding question answered with real facts:
- *What if every employee could stop the company, the way any Toyota worker can stop the line?*
- *What if we swapped jobs — the AI watches, and the human types?* (Bainbridge, reversed.)
- *What if your thermostat lied to itself?* (Model mismatch, via Leveson's Mars Polar Lander.)
- *What if your to-do app needed its own to-do app?* (Turchin's staircase, played for laughs — and it is true of every platform.)
- *How many engineers does it take to keep a platform nobody uses alive?* (Answer: two to five, for years.)

Plus **"Weird Questions from the Inbox"** pages: real reader questions collected through the book's newsletter while it is being written, with credit and permission, a doodle, and no answer. Pure breather.

### C. "In Small Words" boxes (from *Thing Explainer*)
Every chapter's central idea restated using only the thousand most common English words. It is a test of whether the idea is actually understood — and readers love it. Example for chapter 2:

> **In small words:** If you want to keep something working, you need a picture of that thing in your head. If your picture is wrong, you will push the wrong way — and you will push hard, because you think you are right.

### D. The Exploded View — one giant diagram per part (from *Thing Explainer*)
Four big labelled blueprint spreads, one per part of the book, full of dashed-arrow callouts and hidden jokes for people who look closely:
1. **A company as a metasystem** — floors of people, dashboards, the policy layer on the roof, the cord nobody pulls.
2. **Inside a software factory** — agents, specs, holdout scenarios locked in a vault, a human asleep at the review desk.
3. **The ALIS maintenance loop** — aircraft, parts records, the Action Request queue with its fee meter, and the spreadsheet everyone actually trusts.
4. **One person's operating system** — inbox, notes vault, calendar, and the Sunday spent maintaining it all.

### E. Stick-figure comic strips with a recurring cast (from *xkcd*)
Short two-to-six-panel strips, simply drawn, with a small recurring cast:
- **Pat**, a platform engineer who has seen things;
- **Dee**, a manager holding a dashboard that is always green;
- **Unit 7**, an eager AI agent that has never once said "I don't know".

Use the silent-final-panel trick. **Must be original** — xkcd's licence is non-commercial only, so nothing of his can appear in a book for sale.

### F. The Greene template — "The Image" and "The Reversal"
Close every chapter with two short sections:
- **The Image** — one metaphor to remember the chapter by: the dying forest; the cow that warms up before it gets cold; the cord that hangs untouched.
- **The Reversal** — when this chapter's advice is wrong. This is the book's honesty built into its format, and it is exactly what Cook and Perrow demand.

### G. The Rules — numbered, short, shareable (from *The Art of War*)
Each chapter distilled into three to five numbered rules. Collected at the back as **"The Regulator's Rules"**. These double as quote cards for social media. Examples:
- *A dashboard is a model of the work, not the work.*
- *Whoever may stop the line decides what the line produces.*
- *The most reliable automation needs the most practised humans.* (After Bainbridge.)

### H. Author as experiment (from *Moonwalking with Einstein*)
The author runs a real experiment in each part and reports it honestly:
- run a small agentic "software factory" for a month and keep a log;
- live with a single text file as the only productivity system for thirty days;
- ask a willing team to adopt an explicit "anyone may stop the release" rule for a quarter, and interview them after;
- try to explain chapter 2 to three people who have never written code.

This is the cheapest way to create **original first-hand material**, which the research identified as the book's biggest missing piece.

### I. Human units (from Foer)
Convert every big number into something people can feel. Checked against the source before use:
- GAO's 9,262 hours of lost flight time at one base in one year is **more than one aircraft sitting idle for an entire year** (a year has 8,760 hours).
- The mathematicians' declaration: 7,200 signatures is roughly **every seat in a large theatre, filled several times over** — pin to a specific venue when drafting.

### J. "At the Movies" boxes (from the movie-therapy paper)
Films readers already know, used as quick case studies — described in words, since film stills need licences:
- *Office Space* — TPS reports as legibility with nothing behind it.
- *Jurassic Park* — Ian Malcolm on complex, tightly coupled systems: Perrow in a lab coat.
- *Apollo 13* — improvising a filter from what's on board: people creating safety, in Cook's sense.
- *Chernobyl* (HBO) — a safety test that disabled the safety systems.
- *Kung Fu Panda* — a warrior trained to win the scoreboard, not to understand it.

### K. Myth vs Record (already planned; now with a look)
A recurring box with a big red stamp: **MYTH** on the left, **RECORD** on the right, source underneath. The research already supplies five: the 45,000 hours, the $16.7 billion, "18% quit over holacracy", StrongDM's "$1,000 a day", and the Bezos memo nobody has ever seen.

### L. Weird True Things (from *What If?*'s footnotes)
One genuinely surprising, true fact per chapter, set in a margin note. Examples already in the research:
- The US Defense Department **paid a fee every time a mechanic reported a problem** with the F-35's logistics software.
- In 1983 a psychologist described a plant where **management had to stay through the night shift, or the operators would switch the automation off**.
- The best-known productivity study of AI coders had to redesign itself because **developers refused to work without AI, even for $50 an hour**.

### M. "Dispatches from 2031" (from *The Time Machine*)
A one-page piece of clearly labelled fiction opening each part: a report from a near future where the part's lesson was ignored — or, once, where it was learned. Gives the book narrative momentum between the non-fiction sections.

### N. End-of-chapter kit (from *Games of Strategy*)
Summary · Key terms (tiny glossary) · **Try This** (a solved exercise, worked through) · **Your Turn** (an unsolved one, with answers at the back).

## 3. What a chapter looks like, page by page

A typical 25-page chapter:

| Pages | What the reader sees |
|---|---|
| 1 | Chapter title (a provocation, in Manson's style) and one epigraph from history (Greene's "Authority") |
| 2–5 | **Cold open** — the scene. No jokes if it's a serious case. |
| 6 | **The Mechanism** — one diagram in the book's visual grammar |
| 7 | **In Small Words** box |
| 8–14 | The argument, with **Weird True Thing** margin notes and joke footnotes |
| 12 | A **comic strip** |
| 15–19 | **The mirror** — the same thing in software, organisations or your own life, with **In the Field** interview voices |
| 17 | **Myth vs Record** or **At the Movies** box |
| 20–21 | **The Image** and **The Reversal** |
| 22 | **The Rules** (three to five) |
| 23–24 | **Diagnose · Try This · Your Turn** |
| 25 | **The Haiku Line** |

Between some chapters: a **What If?** interlude or a **Weird Questions from the Inbox** page.

## 4. The rules for humour

1. **Every joke carries a fact.** If a joke doesn't teach or help something stick, cut it.
2. **No jokes in the case sections of serious chapters.** Chapter 7 (the Post Office Horizon scandal, with thirteen suicides linked to it) gets **no haiku, no comic, no footnote jokes — none**. The same goes for the 737 MAX, Columbia and Therac-25 sections. The humour elsewhere earns the reader's trust; this is where the book spends it.
3. **Never punch down.** Jokes aim at systems, dashboards, the author, and the reader's own habits — never at the people harmed or the front-line workers.
4. **Rhythm:** about one moment of delight every two pages in the lighter chapters. More makes it a joke book; fewer makes it homework.
5. **Everything original.** The devices above are ideas and free to use. The actual jokes, haikus, comics and diagrams in these books are not. Nothing from them gets copied, traced or closely paraphrased.

## 5. What this adds to the plan

- **Illustration budget grows.** The comic cast and four Exploded View spreads need a real illustrator (or the author, if they can draw — Munroe's style is deliberately simple). The roughly twenty diagrams in `BOOK_PLAN.md` still apply.
- **Two community projects become part of the launch:** the Haiku Wall and the Inbox questions. Both need a newsletter and a simple permission form, and both help build the audience publishers ask about.
- **The author experiments** (section H) close part of the "no original material" gap.
- **Length:** the devices add roughly 10–15% of page count. Keep the word target at 95–110k by trimming argument, not delight.
